EOV Sound Panel

Windows sound in the tray by the clock: microphone, output, mixer and hotkeys

DOWNLOAD FREE PROMO updated 2026-09-27

// About

EOV Sound Panel keeps your everyday Windows sound and microphone settings in one compact window — right in the tray, by the clock. Mute the microphone, move from the headset to the speakers, turn the browser down, switch off auto gain: a click or two, instead of a trip through the Control Panel, the Settings app and the volume mixer. It is built to be the quickest way to the things Windows scatters over three different places.

It does nothing to the sound itself: it reads and writes the very same Windows settings as the built-in windows — only in one place, and labelled so that it is clear what is being changed.

Always in the tray, by the clock

The panel lives in the tray next to the clock and opens with a click on its icon. A middle click mutes the microphone without opening anything; a right click brings up the menu: microphone, speakers, walkie-talkie mode, the manual. The icon also tells you what the microphone is doing: the colour of the circle says whether sound is going through, and the glyph inside says which mode it is in, normal or walkie-talkie.

Microphone and output in one header

Both lanes stay in view on every tab: the device list, a live level meter, a volume slider and a separate power button with a coloured indicator. Picking a device from the list changes the default device in Windows itself, not just inside the program.

Volume in decibels, not only in percent

Percent tells you nothing about whether that is a lot or a little. On a USB microphone 100% can easily mean +21 dB — the input is at its ceiling, and every sound in the house comes through in the pauses. A caption reading "100% · +21 dB" explains the hiss at once; a bare percentage explains nothing.

A volume mixer like Windows', only more honest

The volume of every program that has sound — the same list as Settings → Sound → Volume mixer. It holds every program that has opened sound, even while it is silent: Discord between calls, a browser with a paused video. Each has its own icon; a click on it mutes the program, and the volume changes live while you drag the slider.

The list is gathered from every playback device. When a program is not playing to the selected speakers, its row says so: "plays to Realtek Audio". That is the straight answer to "why can't I hear my browser in the headset".

Hotkeys

Five shortcuts, working from inside any program and with the window hidden: microphone on and off, speakers on and off, show the panel, cycle to the next output device, and a separate walkie-talkie key. You assign them by clicking the field and pressing the keys — the combination is stored straight away.

Walkie-talkie mode

The microphone is open only while a key is held. Release it and you are silent. There is also the reverse, "mute while held": the microphone stays open and the key is there to go quiet quickly — to cough, step away, answer someone at home. When the mode is switched off, the microphone returns to the state it was in before.

Whatever you tweaked can go back

The panel writes settings straight away — which is exactly why it can write them back. As soon as something changes, a bar with an Undo button appears at the bottom of the window and puts everything back at once. And Restore the original sound settings on the System tab returns the devices to what they had before the program first ran — the panel takes that snapshot by itself, before you touch anything. Both buttons ask first.

The mouse wheel does not change values in drop-down lists: you cannot switch a device's format by accident while scrolling a tab.

A resizable window

The panel is narrow, but it stretches vertically by its bottom edge and remembers its height until next time: pull it down and the long tabs no longer need scrolling. The program runs once: starting it again simply brings up the one already running, even from the tray.

The settings nobody wants to dig for

The tabs collect what Windows hides in the device properties: switching off the system processing of the microphone together with its automatic gain, the gain control itself, the default format, exclusive mode, listening to the device. Plus separate default devices for sound and for communications, what the system does during a call, mono audio, and the per-application volume mixer.

What the program does not do

  • no sound processing: no noise suppression, no compressor, no equaliser;
  • no virtual devices, no interference with the audio stream;
  • records nothing and stores no audio;
  • never goes online — not for updates, not for telemetry;
  • rolls nothing back when removed: what you left is what stays.

Being honest about results

When a device simply has no setting — most USB microphones have no gain control, for instance — the row goes grey with "not supported by device" instead of pretending to be a working knob. Settings that live in an administrator-only registry branch are unavailable without rights as well, and the row says so in plain words.

Two limits concern the hotkeys. The keypress is not swallowed and also reaches the active program, so the combination fires in both places. And above a window running as administrator hotkeys do not fire — Windows does not hand its keyboard to ordinary programs. That is a limitation of the system, shared by every program of this kind.
